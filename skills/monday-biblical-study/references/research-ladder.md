# Biblical Study Research Ladder

Use this ladder in order. Stop when the evidence is sufficient for the question;
more sources are not automatically better.

## 1. Establish the text

- Confirm the exact passage boundaries, literary unit, requested translation,
  and genre.
- Read the passage repeatedly in its immediate context; observe repeated words,
  contrasts, speakers, argument flow, and narrative setting.
- Identify the translation used in the final study. For a quoted verse, verify
  the wording against that translation's text.

## 2. Trace Scripture with Scripture

- Use concordances, cross-reference systems, quotations, allusions, and verbal
  links to test genuine canonical connections.
- Read the source and receiving passages in context. A shared word alone does
  not prove an allusion.
- Prefer clear New Testament use of the Old Testament and explicit biblical
  links over clever but unsupported parallels.

## 3. Compare translations and textual notes

- Compare several respected translations when a wording affects interpretation;
  include formal-equivalence and meaning-based translations where useful.
- Consult reputable textual notes for meaningful manuscript variants. Explain
  the variant, its interpretive impact, and what remains unchanged; do not
  manufacture doubt from ordinary translation differences.
- Do not claim the original text is settled where significant scholarly debate
  remains, or make a disputed variant carry the whole study.

## 4. Investigate language carefully

- Use an interlinear, concordance, lexicon, grammar, or scholarly language tool
  only when Greek, Hebrew, or Aramaic materially clarifies the passage.
- Check a term's range of meaning, grammar, immediate syntax, and usage in the
  author and wider canon. Give the original term, transliteration, concise
  gloss, and practical interpretive consequence.
- Avoid etymological fallacies, "hidden meaning" claims, and treating a Strong's
  number or word study as an interpretation by itself.

## 5. Establish historical, cultural, and geographic context

- Use Bible dictionaries, atlases, primary texts, museum or university
  collections, and respected historical scholarship for claims about people,
  places, customs, archaeology, Second-Temple Judaism, or the Greco-Roman world.
- Prefer primary evidence and academically responsible sources. Distinguish
  established evidence, scholarly inference, and disputed proposals.
- Use maps, site plans, or timelines only where geography or sequence changes
  the reader's understanding. Attribute externally sourced visuals and label
  original visuals as schematic.

## 6. Test the reading with commentaries and theological voices

- Consult more than one responsible commentary for consequential interpretive
  claims: ideally a technical or academic source plus a pastorally useful source,
  and an additional tradition when theology or ecclesial practice is disputed.
- Identify meaningful agreement and disagreement; do not create a false
  consensus or select a commentator merely because they support a desired view.
- Use named pastors and teachers as attributed perspectives, not as substitutes
  for exegesis. Quote or paraphrase them faithfully with a specific source.

## 7. Research current claims only when needed

- For current archaeology, scholarship, institutional claims, recent debates,
  or a named contemporary teacher, use current reliable sources and cite them.
- Prefer official archaeological projects, museums, universities, academic
  journals, reputable publishers, and the named person's own published work.
- Treat blogs, search snippets, social posts, and AI-generated summaries as
  leads, not final evidence.

## 8. Synthesize transparently

- Separate **what the text says**, **well-supported context**, **interpretive
  judgment**, **theological inference**, and **application**.
- Name uncertainty and material denominational or scholarly differences.
- Cite sources near consequential historical, linguistic, scholarly, and
  attributed claims. The final study should remain readable; include only
  sources that materially informed it.
- Never represent a tool, commentary, website, or voice as equal to Scripture.
